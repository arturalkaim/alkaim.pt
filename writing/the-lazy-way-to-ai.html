The lazy way to AI

2026-10-19 · 4 min read

I choose a lazy person to do a hard job, because a lazy person will find an easy way to do it.

You've probably seen this one credited to Bill Gates. He most likely never said it, but I don't care much who said it. It has always described me.

I'm lazy. I think that's the main reason I started using AI coding tools so early. If something can write the boring part for me, I want it, and I'll pay for it myself if I have to.

That's how this started.

Paying for autocomplete

I joined the GitHub Copilot preview in 2021. When it went paid in June 2022 I just kept paying.

At first it was slightly smarter autocomplete. Then I noticed that if I wrote the Scaladoc first, properly, with the inputs, the output and the edge cases, Copilot would write the whole method. So I started writing docs first. Less typing for me.

And I started telling people. In February 2023 a colleague's first reaction was: "But Copilot is paid xD". Yes. I was paying for it.

When the company started a Copilot trial in November 2023, I had my message to our CTO ready before the all-hands ended. The reply: I was the first one to ask for a licence, and they could have guessed it would be me. Fair.

Three days vs five minutes

In the summer of 2024 I spent three days stuck on a React problem. ChatGPT didn't solve it, Copilot didn't solve it.

Cursor gave me a working solution at the first try. Five minutes and I was done.

I was in love after that. The same day I was already asking in our AI channel if we could use it.

Paste the ticket and wait

A few months later Windsurf came out and I switched. It was cheaper, and it went further: you give it a goal and it plans, runs commands and edits across files.

On the 29th of November 2024 I tried the laziest thing I could think of. I pasted a ticket into it, a small cleanup to remove a feature toggle, and added "Let's start by creating a branch and then making the changes above".

It created the branch, searched the codebase, found the Scala service with the toggle and started changing it.

I sent the recording to a teammate. They said it was impressive. My answer: "let me check if it runs though 😅"

A few days later I sent it to someone else: "Have you tried this yet?"

Honest about the mess

I never sold it as magic. In March 2025 someone asked me if these tools were coming for our jobs. My answer:

AI generates a lot of code fast, but a lot of it is shitty code. It's perfect for POCs and MVPs, but it just can't handle big complex projects… it breaks more than it makes and needs a lot of guidance.

Both things were true. It made me a lot faster, and if you left it alone it made a mess.

Then Claude Code

In June 2025 I asked in our AI channel: "Opinions on Claude Code?" A month later my weekly update said "Started using Claude Code 🤖".

Honestly, this one was a struggle. I was using it, but I was sceptical.

With Windsurf I still approved every block it changed. I read every line. I just didn't have to type the code, but I had full control of what was going on.

Claude Code was further away from the code. It runs in the terminal, changes a bunch of files, runs the tests and tries again. Much harder to control. A few weeks in, a colleague asked if I was already working on a ticket, because there was an analysis attached to it. My answer: "No, I gave the ticket to Claude Code and it spat that out… haven't even read it yet 😅"

Letting go of that control was hard.

The other thing I didn't expect: I missed the struggle. Working on a hard problem for hours, sometimes days, and finally finding the solution. That dopamine hit was mostly gone. You explain the problem and it just gets fixed.

In my self-review that August, one of the things I wrote under "areas to improve" was "Working with Claude Code". The reason I gave: "It's boring".

Paste the ticket and wait, for real this time. Even for a lazy person, that takes some getting used to.

Still, I pushed to get it for the whole team. I'd paid for my own tools for years, I didn't think everyone else should have to.

Lazy, but honest

Looking back, being lazy taught me two things.

The clearer you are, the less you have to do. A good Scaladoc in 2023, a ticket description in 2024, skills and checklists today. Same idea, better models.

And the best way to get people on board is a short demo on our own code, plus being honest about where it breaks.

What came next

"It breaks more than it makes and needs a lot of guidance."

So I got lazy about the guidance too, and wrote it down so I wouldn't have to repeat it. That turned into the Forge, a pipeline that takes a ticket, investigates, implements, tests, verifies and opens a PR.

But that's the next post.
